Never Enough

Why You Keep Moving the Finish Line

You hit the number. Then the number changed. The finish line wasn't a place. It was a moving target that relocated the moment you got close.

By DOPA  ·  August 2026  ·  5 min read

You hit the number. Then the number changed. You wanted a certain salary — got it, and immediately the "real" number moved higher. You wanted to run a 5K — did it, and the goal quietly became a 10K before you'd even caught your breath. You'd know this pattern has you if you described one of your past achievements the exact same way you'd describe a starting point — if the thing you worked toward for three years sounds, in your mouth, indistinguishable from the entry-level position you were trying to escape.

That's the tell. Not that you're ambitious — ambition is fine. The tell is that you can't point to a moment in the last two years where you actually let something you accomplished count as something you accomplished. The finish line didn't just move. It moved before you could cross it, quietly reclassifying itself so that where you are now is simply where the race begins again.

When the arrival reclassifies itself

There's a psychological mechanism behind this, and it has a name: hedonic adaptation. The brain treats the new normal as the baseline. When you achieve something you've been working toward — a title, a number, a milestone — the brain recalibrates its reference point upward to match. What was once the goal becomes the floor. The emotional lift you expected to feel gets compressed into a shorter and shorter window, and then it's gone. Not because you're ungrateful. Because the brain is doing exactly what it was designed to do in a survival context: once the threat is gone or the resource is secured, recalibrate upward and start scanning for the next threshold. That mechanism kept your ancestors alive. In an achievement context, it's the thing that ensures you can never actually arrive.

This is why research on lottery winners consistently finds that about a year after winning, their baseline happiness has returned to roughly what it was before — and why the same pattern shows up for promotions, salary increases, house upgrades, and most things humans believe will change how they feel. The brain is not interested in savoring the arrival. It is interested in recalibrating to the next problem. The problem is that this happens automatically, invisibly, and without your consent. You don't decide to move the finish line. You wake up one Tuesday morning and discover that it already moved — and that the position you spent a decade chasing now sounds, to your own ears, like "not there yet."

A man spent a decade chasing a specific title at work. Not vaguely — specifically. He had a name in his head for the role, a mental image of what the email signature would look like, a quiet sense that once he had that title, a particular kind of pressure would lift. He got it eighteen months ago. For about two weeks, something settled. He told people about it with a kind of ease he hadn't felt before. Then the recalibration happened, the way it always does — not dramatically, not in a single moment, but gradually, like a tide going out. He now has a new target three levels up, and describes his current, hard-won position the same way he described the entry-level job he started in: as "not there yet." Tuesday mornings feel exactly as pressured as they did when he was still chasing the title. The achievement didn't fail him. The measurement system did.

Two finish lines that kept walking away

A woman set out to save a specific amount before she felt "secure." She'd thought about it carefully. The number wasn't arbitrary — it represented something real to her: the amount that, if her income disappeared tomorrow, would give her two years of breathing room. That felt like the kind of cushion that would let her stop holding her breath. She saved it. She hit the exact number, on a Thursday afternoon, and looked at the account balance for a long moment. What she felt wasn't relief. It was a brief flicker of something — satisfaction, maybe — and then almost immediately a new thought: that's not actually enough given what things cost now, given what might happen, given how long two years actually is. Within a week, "secure" had a new number attached, roughly double the old one. The goal hadn't been irrational. The definition had just shifted.

The problem isn't the ambition — ambition is fine. The problem is using a constantly relocating finish line as the only proof that you're allowed to feel like you've arrived anywhere. The finish line isn't a place. It's a measurement system that has been calibrated, by the brain's own design, to always sit one step ahead of wherever you currently are. What the brain is actually doing is recalibrating "normal" upward after every success — the thing you achieved stops registering as an achievement and starts registering as the new neutral. From neutral, there's only one direction the line can go: higher. And so you start the math again, from a starting point that used to be the finish line, wondering why you can't seem to get ahead.

By that logic, you will never arrive — not because you're failing, but because the system is designed, by definition, to always be one step ahead of you. The finish line is doing exactly what finish lines built this way do. The question isn't how to run faster. The question is whether you're running the right kind of race.

The second measurement you're missing

MyDopa exists to give you a second measurement — not instead of the finish line, alongside it. Not "did I arrive," but "what did I actually do, and does it count." The problem with running entirely on finish-line logic is that the finish line recalibrates automatically, which means the scoreboard is always empty. You never accumulate a record of what you've done, because what you've done gets reclassified as the starting line the moment you do it. A second measurement doesn't erase the goal. It creates a parallel record — one that doesn't reset when the goal moves, one that preserves what actually happened before the brain quietly filed it under "baseline."

That record matters more than it sounds. When you have no evidence of what you've already done — when every past achievement has been reclassified as neutral — your brain draws from an empty well when it tries to assess your capability, your progress, your reasons for confidence. The finish line keeps moving. The record doesn't have to.

One thing to do this week

Write down one goal you've already hit that you quietly reclassified as "not that impressive" once you got there. The promotion, the number, the thing you worked toward for longer than you'd admit. Then write down, honestly, what it actually took to get there — the years, the specific hard days, the version of yourself at the start who would have recognized the end point as a real arrival. Let that sit as its own entry — not folded into the next goal, not used as a prologue to the story of where you need to go next. Just what it was, and what it took.

Do this once, and notice what it feels like to let an achievement be an achievement without immediately recruiting it into the service of the next target. If it feels uncomfortable — if some part of you resists calling the thing you did "enough" — that discomfort is the mechanism running exactly as designed. That's not a character flaw. That's hedonic adaptation doing what it does. The question is whether you want it to run unexamined for another decade, or whether you want to build a second measurement alongside it — one that doesn't move when the finish line does.

You're allowed to keep moving forward. But you're also allowed to log the wins you already earned before the finish line moved again — because otherwise you're running a race with no scoreboard, calling that normal, and wondering why the arrival never feels the way you expected it to.
