Have a Great Yesterday Method

Insight Can Start Change. Why Practice Is What Sustains It.

A book, a conversation, a hard morning can hand you real insight. What happens in the weeks after determines whether it changes anything.

By René Estripeaut  ·  August 2026  ·  6 min read

At thirty-two, in the middle of a personal crisis, a coach handed René a copy of The Power of Now. The book did what good books sometimes do — it gave him language for something he'd been circling for a while without being able to name it clearly: the difference between what actually exists in this moment and the running commentary his mind produced about past and future. It was real insight. It changed how he understood his own thinking, at least for a while.

Then it faded. Not because the idea was wrong. Because nothing in his life was built to hold onto it. There was no practice attached to the insight — no daily action that kept bringing him back to it — so the understanding sat in memory the way most insights do: available if you go looking for it, but no longer doing any work. Life moved on. The book stayed read. The distinction it taught him quietly stopped mattering day to day.

This is the exact pattern the Have a Great Yesterday Method is built around, and it's worth stating precisely, because the imprecise version gets repeated constantly and misses the actual mechanism: insight can start change. Consistent practice is what creates and sustains it. Those are two different jobs, done by two different things, and conflating them is why so much genuine understanding evaporates without ever becoming a different life.

Books, podcasts, courses, conferences, therapy, coaching — all of it can create real value, and none of it should be dismissed as somehow lesser than "true" personal development. The Method doesn't argue that insight is fake or that consuming ideas is a waste of time. It argues something narrower and more useful: the moment of insight is not the same event as the change it makes possible, and treating them as one event is where the trouble starts.

Consider what actually happens inside a moment of insight. Something clicks. A pattern you'd never named suddenly has a name. A behavior you'd always done without examining it gets held up and looked at directly for the first time. That's real — it's not nothing — but it's also, by itself, just information. Information changes what you know. It doesn't automatically change what you do next Tuesday, or the Tuesday after that, when the original clarity has faded and you're back inside the ordinary texture of a normal day with no reminder that anything shifted.

There's a reason a single moment of insight, however genuine, tends not to survive contact with an ordinary week: it has no mechanism for repetition built into it. An insight is an event — it happens once, at a specific time, under specific conditions, and then it's over. A practice is structurally different: it's designed to recur, which means it doesn't depend on the original conditions that produced the insight ever showing up again. You don't need to feel the crisis, or reread the book, or have the coach in the room. You just do the thing, on schedule, and the doing is what keeps the understanding active rather than filed away as something you once knew.

This is where personal-development insight often loses momentum: the realization is treated as the finish line rather than the beginning of a practice. Read the book, have the realization, feel the shift — done. But a realization is the start of a different kind of work, not the completion of it. The work that actually determines whether anything changes is smaller, less dramatic, and almost entirely invisible from the outside: a repeated action, done often enough and specifically enough that it becomes the thing keeping the original insight alive instead of the insight trying to survive on its own.

The relationship between that kind of consistency and self-trust — why the repeated action is what self-trust is actually built on, not the intention behind it — is the argument in Why Self-Trust Comes Before Discipline.

René found this out the slow way. Years after the crisis and the book, around age fifty, he took a mindset course — and this time, instead of just absorbing another idea, he built something around it. A morning practice. Then a nightly one. He started noticing that small, meaningful moments during a day were disappearing before he could hold onto them — a good conversation, a moment of real pride, something someone said that mattered — gone by the time he tried to recall it hours later. So he started capturing them as they happened, in real time, rather than trying to reconstruct them afterward from memory. That single change — capture instead of recall — altered his relationship to his own days in a way the earlier insight, on its own, never had.

The difference between the two experiences is the whole argument. Both involved real understanding. Only one of them had a practice attached to it. The Power of Now gave him a true idea that eventually stopped mattering because nothing sustained it. The daily capture habit gave him a much smaller, much less dramatic action that kept mattering precisely because it repeated — because it didn't depend on remembering the insight, it depended on doing the thing, and doing the thing kept the insight alive as a side effect rather than as the main event.

This reframes what "the Method" is actually offering, which is easy to misread as another insight-delivery system when it's the opposite. The Have a Great Yesterday Method isn't a new idea competing with the ideas already in your head from books and courses and good conversations. It's the daily practice of showing up for yourself — noticing what's real, preserving it, returning to it — regardless of which insight, if any, prompted you to start. The insight is not required and not the point. The practice is what does the sustaining, whether the initial spark came from a therapist, a podcast, a hard year, or nothing identifiable at all.

What "Showing Up for Yourself" Actually Means goes deeper into the governing definition — why the practice is defined by its form and recurrence rather than by the feeling that motivated it.

This connects directly to what the governing definition of the Method actually asks for. Showing up for yourself was never meant as a synonym for having insight, or for caring, or for wanting to change — it means the specific, repeatable action itself, done regardless of whether that day's motivation matches the intensity of the original realization. The morning practice René built wasn't more insightful than reading The Power of Now. It was less insightful, in fact — a few minutes of noticing and writing, nothing philosophically new. What it had that the book didn't was structure: a form specific enough to repeat, on a day when nothing felt clarifying at all, which is most days. That's the entire mechanical difference between an idea that fades and one that becomes durable — not depth of understanding, but whether the understanding has anything attached to it that keeps happening after the understanding itself has stopped feeling new.

What this means concretely is that the question worth asking after any moment of real clarity isn't "did I understand something true." It's "what will I actually do, on a repeatable basis, that keeps this true thing in contact with my life." Without an answer to the second question, the first one — however genuine, however hard-won — has a shelf life measured in days or weeks, not because the insight was shallow, but because nothing was built to carry it past the moment it arrived. With an answer, even a small one, the insight stops needing to be remembered, because it's being lived instead — one small, repeated, unremarkable action at a time.
