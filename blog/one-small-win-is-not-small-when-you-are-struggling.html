The Hard Season

One Small Win Is Not Small When You Are Struggling

Somebody tells you to celebrate the small stuff and it sounds like a participation trophy. But there's a version of small win that has nothing to do with lowering the bar. It has to do with what's actually possible on the days when everything is heavier.

By DOPA  ·  August 2026  ·  5 min read

Somebody tells you to celebrate the small stuff and it lands like an insult. You're not looking for a participation trophy. You're looking for your life to actually move — to feel like things are building toward something, to have evidence that the days are adding up. Being told to celebrate making your bed or sending one email when you're behind on everything, when the gap between where you are and where you want to be is this large — that sounds like the kind of advice people give when they can't think of anything actually useful to say. It sounds like a consolation prize framed as wisdom.

That reaction makes complete sense. The version of "small wins" advice that gets shared most widely is genuinely patronizing — it treats struggle as a mindset problem, and the fix as a matter of choosing to appreciate lesser things. That's not what's being said here. What's being said is something more precise and, once you see it, more useful: the size of a win isn't an absolute measurement. It's a measurement relative to conditions. And when the conditions are hard, the math changes entirely.

Two men, two different kinds of output

A guy in his mid-thirties, going through a separation, made his bed every morning for two weeks straight. He did it before he left for work, in an apartment that was half-empty and quieter than he was used to, in a life that had come apart at most of its load-bearing points simultaneously. He was sleeping four hours a night, sometimes less — lying awake doing the same mental accounting over and over, the kind where you're not reaching any new conclusions but you can't stop running the numbers. He was showing up to work anyway, sitting in meetings, producing functional output, doing the normal surface of his life in a way that looked fine from the outside. Making the bed was the only thing he was doing that he had chosen to do rather than endured. It was a three-minute act in a day that felt mostly like survival. On paper, it's nothing. In context — in the actual context of what it cost him to do it, what it meant to have one thread of order in a life that had come apart everywhere else — it was the evidence that he wasn't in freefall. It was the proof he was still choosing something.

Another man, laid off after eleven years at the same company, applied to one job a day during the three months it took him to find a new position. He had a family to provide for, a mortgage, the particular weight of having believed a job was more stable than it turned out to be. Some days that single application was the total output of his available willpower. He'd sit down, draft it, send it, and feel genuinely depleted in a way he didn't understand — because it was just an application, not physical labor, not a crisis, just an email — but the depletion was real. The job search was running on a fuel tank that anxiety and dread were burning faster than rest was replenishing. Some days he applied to one job and then sat on the couch for an hour, not scrolling, not doing anything in particular, just spent. He sometimes felt ashamed of this. From the outside, one application per day sounds like minimal effort. From inside that month, it was the maximum sustainable output, squeezed from a system operating under load conditions that weren't visible to anyone else.

The mistake in the measurement

The mistake is measuring a hard season by the standards of an easy one. When you're operating at full capacity — sleeping well, not carrying acute stress, with your resources intact — you produce at full capacity. That becomes your mental reference point for what "normal" looks like. Then a hard season hits. Sleep degrades. Cognitive load spikes. The available energy for discretionary effort — the stuff beyond just surviving the day — drops significantly. This isn't weakness. This is what happens to any system running under load conditions it wasn't designed to sustain indefinitely.

But here's the thing: your internal scoreboard doesn't automatically update for the new conditions. You still carry the full-capacity standard as the baseline for what a productive day looks like, what adequate output looks like, what a person who is "doing okay" is supposed to be producing. So when you're at half-output because you're carrying twice the load, the subjective experience isn't "I'm performing well given the conditions." It's "I'm failing to meet the standard." The standard doesn't account for the conditions. It just measures the gap. And in a hard season, that gap is almost always going to look like failure — not because you're failing, but because you're holding yourself to a measurement designed for circumstances you're not currently in.

Context-relative measurement is not about lowering the bar. It's about measuring the right thing. If you bench-press 200 pounds and you want to know whether that's impressive, the answer depends entirely on how much you weigh, how long you've been training, and what you were lifting before. An absolute number without context tells you very little. The same logic applies to what you get done in a day. Output measured against full-capacity baseline, when you're in a hard season, systematically undercounts what you're actually doing — which means it produces a false picture of how you're performing, which then feeds a story about your competence and your trajectory that isn't accurate.

What MyDopa is actually built for

This is the specific gap MyDopa is built for: not to inflate wins that don't matter, but to make sure the wins that are actually enormous — given the conditions you were operating under — don't get filed away as nothing just because they'd look small on an easier week. The tool is built around logging what you did and what it cost you, not just what it looks like in isolation. Because the thing you did while sleeping four hours a night and managing a life crisis is not the same as the thing you'd do on a rested Tuesday with nothing pressing. They look the same from the outside. They are not the same from the inside. The record should reflect that difference.

Over time, that record does something else: it gives you a legible version of the hard season rather than a blank spot. Hard seasons are almost always invisible in retrospect — they collapse in memory to a vague sense that things were bad, and the specifics of what you actually did to get through them dissolve. The things you produced, the decisions you made, the small acts of maintenance and survival and forward motion — they disappear from the account. You're left with the feeling of the season without the evidence of who you were in it. The log prevents that erasure. It keeps the evidence of what you chose to do, at cost, during the period when the cost was real.

One thing to do this week

Every night, write down the one thing you did that day that took more out of you than it would have on a normal day. Not the biggest thing — not the most impressive thing — the thing that cost the most, given what you were carrying. If getting out of bed and making it to one scheduled commitment was the thing that cost everything, write that. If sending the single email you'd been dreading was the maximum available output, write that. Don't filter by what looks impressive. Filter by what was actually hard, for you, today, under today's conditions. Track the cost, not just the output.

Do this for two weeks and two things will happen. First, you'll start to see that you have, in fact, been doing things — specific things, on specific days, at real cost. The hard season won't look as blank from the inside. Second, you'll develop a more accurate sense of your baseline, and the gap between the full-capacity standard and where you actually are will feel less like personal failure and more like what it actually is: a measurement of current conditions, not permanent capacity.

You are not weak for struggling with things that used to be easy. You are not behind the version of yourself who existed before the hard season started. You're operating a system under different load conditions, and the system is still producing. That's not small — that's the entire job right now. The only measurement that matters in a hard season is whether you're still moving, still choosing, still doing the next thing. By that measurement, you're already doing it.
