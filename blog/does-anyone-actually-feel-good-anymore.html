Life Script Collapse

Does Anyone Actually Feel Good Anymore?

Not existential dread. Not crisis. Just a low-level flatness that wasn't there ten years ago. If you're wondering whether this is normal, you're not alone.

By DOPA  ·  August 2026  ·  6 min read

Not depressed. Not in crisis. Not even particularly unhappy, if someone asks. Just — not quite fine, in a way that's hard to put words to. A low-level flatness that wasn't there ten years ago, or was there but less constant. A background noise of "is this it" that runs during otherwise perfectly acceptable days. You're doing the things. The things are not delivering the way they were advertised.

This is not clinical. It's not something you'd bring to a doctor. It's more like a collective ambient experience that seems to be everywhere and that almost nobody talks about directly — they talk around it, with words like "burnout" or "overwhelm" or "I just need a vacation," but the vacation doesn't fix it and neither does the next goal, and the word "burnout" doesn't quite capture something that's been running at this temperature for three years rather than spiking and resolving.

What the life script promised

There was a script. It was fairly coherent and fairly universal for the generation that grew up with it: work hard enough in school, find the right career path, make progress, accumulate the markers of a good life, eventually arrive somewhere that feels stable and satisfying. The script was not entirely wrong as a description of how institutions are designed to work. It was wrong about what arriving at those markers feels like from the inside.

The markers don't feel like arrival. They feel like terrain. You get the job and then you have a job. You get the relationship and then you're in a relationship. You get the apartment and then you live in an apartment. The thing you worked for becomes the container for the next thing you're working for, and the felt sense of "I did it" that was supposed to come with each achievement either doesn't arrive or arrives briefly and then leaves, depositing you in the same internal space you were in before. The script didn't mention this part.

When You Have More Tools But Feel Less Alive

The optimization paradox

You have access to more tools, more information, more frameworks for improving your life than any generation before you. There are apps for sleep, for productivity, for meditation, for tracking your mood, for tracking your habits, for optimizing your nutrition. There are podcasts on the neuroscience of focus. There are books on the research behind every behavioral change you might want to make. There is, in short, an enormous infrastructure built around helping you feel better.

The paradox is that more tools has not translated into more ease. If anything, the proliferation of tools for feeling better creates a new problem: if you have all of this and still don't feel good, what does that say about you? The not-feeling-good begins to carry an additional weight — the sense that you're doing something wrong, that you're failing to implement correctly, that someone somewhere is using these tools and experiencing the ease they promised and you just haven't figured out how to get there yet. The lack of ease becomes evidence of personal failure rather than evidence that the tools were oversold.

Why Millennials Feel Numb

Two people in the ambient feeling

A woman had what she would describe as "a good life by any reasonable measure." She said this sincerely. She was not performing contentment — she genuinely found things to appreciate, had moments of real feeling, genuinely cared about the people around her. What she also had was a persistent background question of whether this — the specific texture of the life she was living — was what having a good life felt like, or whether something had been calibrated wrong somewhere. She didn't have a name for what she was missing. She wasn't sure she was missing anything, exactly. She was sure the question was real.

A man had hit most of the goals he'd set at thirty. At thirty-eight, he could look at the list and check most of it — the professional position, the house, the family, the financial stability he'd wanted. He felt, more often than he expected, a mild dissatisfaction that he was embarrassed to admit because the life was objectively what he'd aimed for. The dissatisfaction wasn't ingratitude. It was the gap between how he'd imagined arrival would feel and how arrival actually felt, which was mostly like being somewhere with a new set of responsibilities rather than a destination you finally inhabit.

What might actually be happening

The ambient flatness isn't usually evidence that something is wrong with the life or the person. It's more often evidence that the model was wrong — specifically, the part of the model that located the good feeling in the outcome rather than in the living. Humans are not particularly good at predicting how things will feel once they arrive. We systematically overestimate how much the good things will affect our mood and underestimate how quickly we adapt to them. This is one of the better-replicated findings in the psychology of wellbeing.

What this means practically is that the feeling you were chasing isn't actually located in the destination. It's located in a different kind of engagement with the life you're already in — the kind that notices specific real moments as they're happening and lets them land, rather than moving through them toward the next thing on the list. That sounds obvious when stated directly. It's not obvious in practice, because the orientation toward the next thing is the orientation you've been trained in since school.

One thing to do this week

Instead of asking "why don't I feel better?" — which typically generates explanations and not felt experience — try asking something more specific: what was one moment today that was actually good? Not transcendent. Not the highlight reel. Just actually good. The coffee that tasted right, the conversation that landed, the moment the problem at work clicked into place. Then stay with it for twelve seconds before moving on.

Not to manufacture contentment. Not to practice positivity. But to give the actual good moment the encoding time it needs to register as a data point in your record of the day. The ambient flatness is partly a measurement problem — you're running on a record that systematically underweights the real good in favor of the ongoing and the concerning. One accurate data point per day doesn't fix that record. But it starts a more accurate one. And a more accurate record, over time, changes what you believe about whether the days are good.
