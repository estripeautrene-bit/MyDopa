Arrival Fallacy

Recognizing Arrival Fallacy in High-Achieving Clients

Some of the flattest clients you will work with are the ones who just succeeded. The promotion landed, the target was hit, the outcome they had organized their life around finally arrived — and what they bring into the room is a quiet, hard-to-place deflation.

By DOPA  ·  August 2026  ·  7 min read

It is easy to miss, because it does not present as a problem. The client is functioning, often exceptionally. Nothing is wrong on paper. What they describe, if they can name it at all, is a sense that the achievement did not deliver what it seemed to promise — and a private worry that something must be wrong with them for feeling so little at the top of something they worked years to reach.

For practitioners across coaching, therapy, and performance work, learning to recognize this pattern early is useful, because it responds poorly to the obvious interventions and well to a specific reframe.

What the pattern is

The popular term is the arrival fallacy — a phrase brought into wide use in positive psychology by Tal Ben-Shahar to describe the mistaken expectation that reaching a goal will produce lasting well-being. The client attaches an enduring emotional state to a future attainment, reaches it, and experiences a spike that decays within days.

Underneath it sits a well-documented mechanism: hedonic adaptation. Research on affective forecasting and adaptation has consistently found two things that matter here. People overestimate how long the emotional impact of a positive event will last, and they habituate to improved circumstances, returning toward a stable baseline. The affect generated during goal striving is also, in much of the literature, distinct from the affect at goal attainment — the pursuit carries more anticipatory charge than the achievement carries lasting satisfaction.

A structural feature, not a phase

For high achievers, the arrival fallacy is not an occasional disappointment. It is a structural feature of how they operate — a life organized around attainments, each of which adapts away, leaving the baseline unmoved and the next target already selected.

The client did not fail to feel enough because the achievement was too small. They failed to feel enough because attainment was never the variable carrying the feeling.

Why high performers are especially exposed

Three factors concentrate this pattern in high-achieving populations.

First, milestone density. High performers structure their lives almost entirely around discrete, high-stakes outcomes. This gives adaptation frequent, sharp edges to work on — spike, decay, reset, repeat — and very little else on the emotional ledger between milestones.

Second, identity fusion with outcomes. When self-worth is closely tied to attainment, the post-achievement flatness is not read as a normal adaptation curve. It is read as a threat to identity — if hitting the goal didn't fix it, maybe nothing will — which raises the stakes on the next target and accelerates the loop.

Third, an impoverished record of process. High performers are typically excellent at recording outcomes and poor at registering the daily evidence of who they are becoming. Their scoreboard is milestones-only. Between the milestones, which is nearly all of the time, the scoreboard reads empty — the same blind spot that keeps moving the finish line just out of reach no matter how much is accomplished.

The result is a client who is, by external measures, thriving, and who is quietly certain their success did not make them happy.

What tends not to help — and what does

Two common moves tend to backfire. Encouraging a positive-reframing exercise alone often reads to these clients as instruction to feel something they don't, adding a layer of failure. And helping them set a bigger, better goal simply reloads the mechanism — a larger attainment adapts away just as reliably as a smaller one, on the same schedule.

Setting a more meaningful goal for a client caught in arrival fallacy treats the symptom as the cure. The problem is not the destination. It is the exclusive reliance on destinations.

What tends to help is shifting the client's source of felt progress from attainment to accumulation — from the rare milestone to a visible, daily record of process. This maps directly onto the anticipatory-versus-consummatory distinction: the anticipation built into daily building is a different, more durable driver than the spike of arrival. When a client can see the line of their own consistent action stacking up, they gain a well-spring of felt progress that does not depend on the next attainment and does not adapt away in the same manner, because it is continuously replenished rather than delivered once.

Practically, this means helping the client build an evidence record: a low-friction daily practice of capturing what they actually did and who they are becoming, held somewhere visible enough to accumulate. It is the same shift a client discovers when they finally admit that reaching everything they wanted left the baseline untouched — and start measuring the process instead.

Where MyDopa fits

MyDopa is built on precisely this shift. It captures the daily evidence of progress the mind discards and makes it visible over time, so a person's sense of movement rests on an accumulating record rather than the next milestone. For clients caught in the arrival loop, that visible record supplies the continuous felt progress that attainment keeps failing to deliver — a complement to the reflective work you are already doing, not a replacement for it.

The practitioner's contribution is the reframe and the relationship. The tool's contribution is keeping the evidence so the reframe has something concrete to stand on between sessions.

A note on framing with clients

Language matters with this population. Framing the work as fixing a deficit tends to land poorly with high performers who are accustomed to competence. Framing it as correcting a measurement error — you have been keeping score on milestones only, and milestones are almost never today; let's start counting the rest — tends to land well, because it locates the problem in the scoreboard rather than the person. That framing is accurate, and it opens the door to the daily-evidence practice that actually moves the baseline.
