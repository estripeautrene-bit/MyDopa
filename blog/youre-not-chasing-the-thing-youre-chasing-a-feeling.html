Anticipation Trap

You're Not Chasing the Thing. You're Chasing a Feeling.

You wanted the job. You got it — and three weeks in, you were already looking at the next one. The wanting disappeared faster than the having arrived.

By DOPA  ·  August 2026  ·  5 min read

You wanted the job. Then you got it, and three weeks in, you were already looking at the next one. You wanted the apartment. You got it, unpacked the last box, and within a month it just felt like where you lived. The wanting disappeared faster than the having arrived — which is unsettling the first time you notice it, and quietly demoralizing by the fifth or sixth time, because by then you've started to wonder whether you're capable of actually being satisfied by anything, or whether there's something structurally broken in the way you experience what you have.

This is one of the most common quiet complaints there is — the sense that you keep arriving and the arrival keeps feeling like less than you thought it would. And because it's quiet, because it doesn't look like a crisis from the outside, most people never name it directly. They just keep chasing. They assume the next one will be the one that holds the feeling. The pattern repeats. The destinations accumulate. The satisfaction doesn't.

The mechanism nobody explains

Here's what's actually happening, and why nobody explains it: dopamine isn't the reward chemical. It's the anticipation chemical. Your brain's dopamine system evolved not to signal that you've gotten something good, but to signal that something good is coming — and to drive behavior accordingly. Dopamine spikes hardest in the reaching, not the arriving. This is why the version of you that was working toward the thing was, neurochemically, having a better time than the version of you who finally got it. The dopamine was doing its job in the reaching phase. Once the thing arrived, the signal dropped — because the reaching was over, and reaching is what the signal was there to sustain.

The mechanism runs deeper than that. Neuroscientists describe it in terms of prediction error: your brain is constantly generating predictions about what will happen next and comparing them against what actually happens. When the outcome matches the prediction — when you get the thing you worked for, exactly as expected — the dopamine signal stays flat. There's no error to correct. No new information. The brain already priced in the outcome. What generates the dopamine spike is the gap between expected and better-than-expected — the surprise, the uncertainty, the not-yet. Which means the moment the outcome is certain, the most reliable source of the feeling is already gone. You haven't arrived at satisfaction. You've arrived at the point where the chemical driver of satisfaction has nothing left to do.

Hedonic adaptation compounds this. Even when arrival initially delivers some version of the feeling you were chasing, the brain recalibrates to the new normal with uncomfortable speed. The promotion becomes the baseline within weeks. The apartment becomes just where you live. The new relationship becomes the familiar relationship. This isn't ingratitude or character failure — it's the brain doing exactly what it was built to do, which is to stop allocating attention to things that are stable and redirect it toward things that are uncertain, changing, still in motion. The brain's job is to navigate the future, not to savor the present. And it's relentlessly good at its job.

Two arrivals that couldn't hold the weight

A man spent eight months planning a trip to Japan — not daydreaming, but genuinely planning: researching neighborhoods, saving hundreds of photos, building a color-coded spreadsheet of restaurants organized by area and cuisine, watching documentaries about places he'd earmarked. The planning was, in retrospect, the main event. The dopamine was living in the spreadsheet, in the research, in the gap between where he was and where he was going. The trip itself was good. The food was real, the temples were quieter than he expected, the train system was genuinely remarkable. But it was not eight-months-of-planning good. It couldn't be. Nothing could be, because the eight months of planning had been spending a currency that the arrival could only partially redeem. He came home vaguely disappointed in himself for being vaguely disappointed, unsure how to explain that the actual experience was fine but somehow less than the experience of anticipating it.

A woman chased a promotion for two years — genuinely worked for it, put in the extra hours, had the difficult conversations, made herself visible in ways that cost her. She hit it. The Friday she got the email confirming the title change, she felt it — real satisfaction, the thing she'd been working toward. By the following Tuesday, the feeling had evaporated. Not diminished. Evaporated. Replaced, almost immediately and without her choosing it, by a new target she'd barely been aware of before — a different level, a different scope, a different version of the next thing. She hadn't done anything wrong. She'd done everything right, including feeling genuinely good about the win. The problem wasn't her capacity for satisfaction. It was that satisfaction is metabolized fast, and the next anticipation cycle was already running before she'd finished the last one.

Working with the chase instead of fighting it

The standard advice at this point is some version of "practice gratitude" or "be present" — which isn't wrong exactly, but it's like being told to fight your nervous system with good intentions. The dopamine system is ancient. It is not interested in your gratitude practice. What actually works is understanding what the system is doing and building around it rather than against it. If anticipation is where the feeling lives, and arrival is where it dissipates, then the practice isn't to stop chasing — it's to stop treating the chase as a problem to be solved and start treating it as information about where the feeling actually is.

This is what MyDopa is built to do. Not to flatten the chase or convince you to stop wanting things — but to make the process itself visible and recorded, so the wins along the way get the same weight you've been unconsciously giving only to the arrival. When you log a difficult conversation you had this week, a decision you made, a skill you practiced, you're capturing the moments where the dopamine was actually running — the live edge of the chase, where you were still in motion, still uncertain, still becoming. The finish line isn't where the feeling is. The log is closer to it than the destination is. And a record of those moments, built over time, starts to show you something the arrival never could: what your forward motion actually looks like from the inside.

One thing to do this week

Pick one thing you're currently working toward. Not the finish line — the thing you're in the middle of right now. Each day this week, write down one specific moment from that process: a conversation you had that moved it forward, a decision you made under uncertainty, something you chose to do even though the outcome wasn't guaranteed. Don't write the goal. Write the action. Treat the action as the win, not proof that you're getting closer to a win. You are already in the territory where the feeling lives — the practice is just learning to log it instead of looking past it toward the next arrival.

Do this for seven days and something subtle shifts. Not the chase itself — that doesn't stop, and it shouldn't. But your relationship to it changes when you have evidence that you were already doing the thing you were chasing the feeling for. The log becomes a record of the reaching, which is the record of the feeling itself. That's more durable than any arrival.

You are not broken for feeling less than you expected once you arrived. You were just looking for the feeling in the wrong place. It was never at the finish line — it was in the reaching, in the uncertainty, in the moment when the outcome still mattered because it wasn't certain yet. That's where you already spend most of your life. The question is whether you're paying attention to it while it's happening, or only measuring it once it's over — when the feeling is already gone.
