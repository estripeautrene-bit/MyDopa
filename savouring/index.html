The Registration Problem

Modern life is extremely good at producing movement and comparatively poor at producing registration. People scroll, react, switch tasks, plan ahead, consume, optimize, and move on, often within the same experience that was supposed to be enjoyable in its own right. This pattern does not spare positive experiences. A good moment is frequently treated as a transition between the thing that just happened and the thing coming next, rather than as something worth staying in for its own sake. The event occurred. Whether it was actually registered, in a way that makes it more available to remember and reflect on later, is a separate question entirely, and the honest answer is often no.

This is not primarily a claim about a chaotic modern environment, although that environment makes the problem more acute. It is a more basic observation about attention: a positive experience only becomes part of a person's usable record if enough attention was given to it while it was happening or immediately afterward. Attention affects what people notice, what they remember, and what remains available for later reflection. When attention moves on too quickly, the experience can still have occurred without leaving much behind.

What Savouring Means

Savouring is the act of fully noticing, registering, extending, and preserving the experience of something good while it is happening or immediately after it happens. It is not a synonym for gratitude, and treating the two as interchangeable would blur exactly the distinction that makes this concept useful. Gratitude is generally about appreciating what is good — recognizing that something in a person's life deserves to be valued. Savouring is more specific and more behavioral: it is about the quality of attention given to a particular experience while that experience is still live. A person can be deeply grateful for their family in the abstract while still rushing through dinner without fully noticing the moment. Gratitude and savouring can coexist, but neither guarantees the other.

Savouring also differs from mindfulness, though the two are frequently confused. Mindfulness, in most common usage, describes a general orientation toward present-moment awareness, often practiced independently of whether anything particularly good is happening. Savouring is narrower and more targeted: it applies specifically to positive or meaningful experiences, with the purpose of giving them enough attention to register rather than simply observing whatever is occurring without judgment. And savouring differs from journaling, which typically happens after the fact, as a way of recording or processing something already over. Savouring is concerned with the experience itself, in the moment it is happening or in the narrow window immediately afterward, before the moment has fully receded.

Why Good Experiences Disappear

Good experiences disappear for reasons that are almost the opposite of why problems tend to stick. A person's attention is often already on the next task by the time a good moment is underway, because the moment did not announce itself as requiring attention the way a problem does. A meaningful experience can feel too ordinary to register as worth pausing for — a specific look from a spouse, an offhand comment from a friend, ten unremarkable minutes with a child that turn out to matter more than most of the day. People frequently assume they will remember something later simply because it felt significant at the time, without noticing that the feeling of significance and the act of actually preserving the experience are not the same thing.

Achievement is a particularly clear example. A person can complete something genuinely difficult and, within minutes, already be oriented toward the next goal, with barely any attention given to the achievement itself before it becomes background. Praise gets discounted almost reflexively, deflected before it has a chance to land. A meaningful interaction gets swallowed by whatever else is happening that day, simply because nothing structured the moment enough to stand out from the rest of it. None of this requires a person to be careless or ungrateful. It only requires ordinary attention operating the way it usually does, moving toward whatever is next rather than staying with whatever just happened.

Why Achievement Alone Is Not Enough

Achieving something and fully registering that achievement are two different events, and the gap between them explains a pattern many people recognize without having a name for it: reaching a goal and feeling strangely little in the moment, or barely being able to recall the feeling later even though the accomplishment was real. This happens because attention had already shifted to the next target before the current one was given room to register. The achievement is not diminished by this — it still happened, and it still counts as real progress — but its availability for later reflection depends on whether it was actually attended to, not merely completed.

This is a different problem from the one addressed elsewhere in the MyDopa™ system. Fall in Love With Your Own Progress is about recognizing the accumulated evidence of a life and understanding it as genuinely one's own. That recognition depends on evidence being available to look back on in the first place. Savouring happens earlier in the sequence: it asks whether a meaningful experience was fully noticed at the time it occurred, before there is anything to look back on at all. A rough sequence can help clarify how these ideas relate without collapsing into one another: savouring helps a moment register, capture preserves it, visibility lets it accumulate, and personal progress gives the accumulated record its meaning. Each step depends on the one before it, and none of them substitute for each other.

Savouring in Ordinary Life

Savouring rarely requires a dramatic moment to apply to. It can look like noticing a good moment while it is actually happening rather than only recognizing it afterward. It can look like lingering with a feeling of pride for a few extra seconds after finishing something difficult, instead of moving immediately to what comes next. It can look like fully receiving a piece of praise — actually taking it in — rather than deflecting it out of habit. It can look like sharing a positive experience with someone else shortly after it happens, which tends to extend and reinforce the experience rather than merely reporting it. It can look like briefly revisiting a meaningful moment later the same day, or capturing some trace of it before the rest of the day overwrites it entirely.

This matters most for the moments that are small and non-repeatable. A major achievement is often remembered automatically, simply because of its size and rarity. A ten-minute conversation with a child, an unexpected compliment from a colleague, a particular quality of light on an ordinary walk, or the quiet relief of finally finishing something that had been weighing on someone — these are the moments most likely to disappear entirely unless attention is intentionally given to them, because nothing about their scale forces that attention on its own.

Where MyHGY Fits

MyHGY already contains a specific practice built around this idea: noticing something good while it is happening and writing it down immediately, before the day moves on and the moment loses its specificity. Savouring is the broader mechanism underneath that practice, not a duplicate of it. MyHGY is one concrete way of doing what savouring describes; savouring is the underlying act of giving a positive or meaningful experience enough attention for it to register, which can take other forms as well — pausing with a feeling, receiving praise fully, or sharing an experience with someone else in the moment it happens. MyHGY sits inside this broader territory as a specific, deliberate practice, not as the entirety of what savouring covers.

Where MyDopa™ Fits

Why This Matters Personally

The problem savouring addresses is not always that a person's life lacks good moments. Often the moments are genuinely there. The problem is that a good moment did not stay long enough in attention to become part of a usable record, so it functions as though it never happened at all when a person later tries to recall evidence of a good life, a meaningful relationship, or real progress. A life can contain far more good material than a person's memory of it would suggest, simply because most of that material passed through too quickly to register on the way in.

Why This Matters Professionally

Therapists, coaches, mentors, and educators regularly work with people who arrive able to describe their problems in detailed, specific terms while struggling to recall positive evidence from the same period with anything like the same precision. This is not because the positive evidence did not exist. It is often because it was never registered clearly enough to be recalled on demand, while problems, by their nature, tend to command attention whether or not anyone intends to remember them. A practice of savouring, paired with some form of capture, can give positive experiences — a successful response to a difficult situation, a moment of genuine connection, a feeling of competence or relief — somewhere to remain, so a client arrives at the next conversation with more than a lopsided record weighted entirely toward what went wrong. This does not position savouring as treatment or suggest that it replaces professional judgment. It offers the same value described elsewhere in the MyDopa™ system: better evidence to think about together, in this case evidence that would otherwise have disappeared before either person had a chance to work with it.

The Category

A good experience can happen to a person without ever fully becoming part of them, not because the experience was insignificant, but because it was never given enough attention to register. Savouring™ names the deliberate interruption of that pattern: staying with a meaningful positive experience long enough — while it is happening or immediately after — for it to become something a person can actually carry forward, rather than something that occurred and was gone before it had the chance to count.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is savouring?

Savouring is the act of fully noticing, registering, extending, and preserving a meaningful positive experience while it is happening or immediately afterward, so the experience becomes available for later reflection rather than passing through unnoticed.

Why do good experiences sometimes disappear so quickly?

Because attention frequently moves on to the next task or goal before a positive experience has been given enough attention to register. The experience still occurs, but without sufficient attention at the time, little of it remains available to recall later.

How is savouring different from gratitude?

Gratitude is about appreciating that something in a person's life is good. Savouring is about the quality of attention given to a specific experience while it is occurring. A person can feel grateful in general while still failing to savour a particular moment as it happens.

How is savouring different from mindfulness?

Mindfulness generally describes a broad orientation toward present-moment awareness, applied regardless of whether anything positive is occurring. Savouring is narrower: it applies specifically to positive or meaningful experiences, with the purpose of helping them register rather than simply observing the present moment.

How is savouring different from journaling?

Journaling typically happens after an experience is already over, as a way of recording or processing it. Savouring is concerned with the experience itself, in the moment it is happening or in the brief window immediately after, before the moment has fully receded.

Can you savour an achievement?

Yes. Achieving something and fully registering that achievement are separate events. A person can complete something significant and move immediately to the next goal without giving the achievement itself much attention, which is why savouring an achievement — pausing with it briefly before moving on — matters even when the achievement itself is not in question.

Can small everyday moments be worth savouring?

Yes, and these are often the moments most worth deliberate attention, because their small scale means nothing forces them to be remembered automatically. A brief conversation, an unexpected compliment, or an ordinary moment of connection can disappear entirely unless some attention is intentionally given to it.

What is the relationship between savouring and memory?

Attention affects what a person notices, what they remember, and what remains available for later reflection. Savouring increases the likelihood that a positive experience leaves something behind in memory by giving it deliberate attention while it is still occurring, rather than relying on the experience to register itself.

How does MyHGY support savouring?

MyHGY includes a specific practice of noticing something good while it is happening and writing it down immediately. This is one concrete application of savouring, not the entirety of the concept, which also includes other forms of giving attention to a positive experience as it occurs.

How does MyDopa™ support savouring?
