Feelings Are Not Facts™

Feelings are real. Feelings are data. Feelings are not necessarily facts — and the distance between those three statements is where a great deal of unnecessary distortion happens in how people judge their own lives.

Feelings Are Not Facts™ is the principle that emotions are real experiences and valuable information, while the conclusions drawn from them should be tested against the evidence of what has actually happened.

A feeling can arrive with total conviction. In the middle of a hard week, the thought "I am getting nowhere" does not feel like one interpretation among several. It feels like a direct report on reality, as certain as the weather outside the window. This is the territory Feelings Are Not Facts™ addresses: the gap between the intensity of an emotional experience and the accuracy of the conclusion a person draws from it.

The phrase itself is not new, and MyDopa™ did not originate it. Versions of this idea have circulated for decades in recovery communities, therapy, and popular psychology, usually as a corrective to the assumption that strong emotion is automatically reliable information about the world. What MyDopa™ contributes is not the phrase but a specific architecture for what to do about it: the idea that a preserved record of a person's actual life can sit alongside a current feeling and give that feeling something concrete to be checked against, rather than leaving the feeling to stand as the only available evidence.

The core distinction is straightforward to state and easy to lose sight of in practice. A feeling is a genuine internal experience, and it often contains real information worth attending to. But the strength or certainty of a feeling does not automatically make the interpretation attached to it objectively accurate. "I feel like I am failing" describes something real happening inside a person. It is not, by itself, evidence that failure is actually occurring. "I feel rejected" is a real experience. What it means about the other person, or about oneself, is a separate question that the feeling alone cannot answer. This is not a claim that feelings are unreliable in general or that they should be dismissed. It is a narrower and more useful claim: feelings and the conclusions built on top of them are two different things, and treating them as identical is where the trouble starts.

Feelings Are Real

None of this works if it becomes a case against emotion. Feelings are not a malfunction to be corrected or a distraction from clear thinking. They are a genuine form of experience, and in many cases they are tracking something worth noticing — a boundary crossed, a mismatch between values and circumstances, a signal that something needs attention. Treating a feeling as unreal or unimportant does not make its underlying signal disappear; it just removes a source of information a person might otherwise have used well. The starting position here has to be respect for the experience itself.

Feelings Are Data, Not Verdicts

The more useful frame is to treat a feeling as data rather than as a verdict. Data is something to be considered alongside other data, weighed, and interpreted in context. A verdict is final: it closes the question rather than opening it. When a feeling operates as a verdict, whatever it asserts is treated as already settled, and no further evidence gets consulted, because none seems necessary. When a feeling operates as data, it becomes one input among several, worth taking seriously without being treated as the entire case. The shift from verdict to data does not weaken the feeling's importance. It changes what the feeling is allowed to conclude on its own.

Why a Feeling Can Feel Like the Whole Truth

There is a well-studied pattern in psychology called emotional reasoning, a term David Burns identified and named in his work cataloguing common cognitive distortions, building on Aaron Beck's foundational research into the automatic thought patterns underlying depression. The pattern is sometimes summarized as "I feel it, therefore it must be true." It describes exactly the gap this page is concerned with: an emotional state gets treated as though it were direct, self-evident proof of the situation it appears to be about, rather than one perspective on that situation formed under particular conditions. This is well established territory in cognitive behavioral approaches to thought and mood, and it exists precisely because emotional states are convincing from the inside. A feeling does not present itself as an interpretation open to question. It presents itself as the way things are.

Part of what makes this pattern so persistent is that mood shapes what a person notices and recalls in the moment, an effect Gordon Bower's research on mood and memory documented directly: people recall a higher proportion of memories congruent with their current mood, and mood shifts what gets noticed in the first place, not only what gets remembered afterward. A difficult stretch tends to bring difficult memories and difficult predictions more readily to mind, which then appear to confirm the mood that surfaced them in the first place. This is not a claim about brain chemistry or a diagnosis of anything; it is a description of an ordinary, well-documented pattern in how attention and memory work under emotional pressure. A person in a low mood is not imagining their difficulties, but they may be working from a narrower, more selectively negative set of evidence than actually exists, simply because that is the material mood tends to surface first.

Experience, Interpretation, and Evidence

Experience → Interpretation → Evidence. It helps to separate three things that ordinarily arrive fused together into one event: the experience itself, the interpretation attached to it, and the evidence available to test that interpretation. The experience is simply what a person feels — discouraged, afraid, ashamed, defeated. The interpretation is the claim that experience seems to support — I am failing, I never finish anything, nothing is working, I always end up here. The evidence is what has actually happened: the specific things a person has done, handled, built, and lived through, independent of how any particular day happens to feel.

These three are not naturally kept apart. The experience arrives, the interpretation rides in immediately behind it feeling like part of the same event, and the evidence — if it is even considered — gets evaluated through the lens the interpretation has already set up. Separating them deliberately is the actual work this page is describing. It does not require dismissing the experience or arguing yourself out of how you feel. It requires noticing that the interpretation is a separate claim, one that can be checked against something other than the feeling that produced it.

What Happens When the Feeling Becomes the Whole Record

The clearest cost of collapsing these three into one shows up when the current feeling is the only record a person has to consult. If nothing has been preserved from the weeks or months before this one, then whatever the present moment feels like becomes, by default, the entire available account of how things have been going. A person who is having a genuinely difficult week, but who has also spent the previous several months handling real difficulty well, following through on real commitments, and making real progress, may have none of that available to set against the current feeling. Not because it did not happen, but because nothing kept it accessible. The feeling wins the argument by default, not because it is more accurate, but because it is the only witness in the room.

Preserved Evidence and a Fuller Picture

This is where a preserved record of a person's actual life becomes useful: additional testimony the feeling does not otherwise have to compete with, on its own, as the only account available. The feeling says: I am getting nowhere. A preserved record might show three difficult conversations that were actually handled, a project that moved forward despite real obstacles, a commitment kept when it would have been easier to drop it, a setback that was recovered from rather than surrendered to, and several small, easy-to-overlook signs of forward motion. None of this proves the feeling is false, and it is not meant to win an argument against the emotion or talk a person out of what they are experiencing. A decision about what is actually true of a person's life should be made from more than a single, mood-selected sample, when a fuller record is available to consult.

This is also precisely why the concept sits upstream of accuracy in the broader sequence this system describes. The feeling is usually valid as an experience; the more useful question to ask alongside it is how accurate the interpretation being built from that feeling actually is, right now. A more complete record of evidence supports a more accurate answer to that question. A more accurate picture makes patterns easier to see, which is what clarity means. And clarity of that kind is what allows self-confidence to rest on something more durable than the mood of the current day — a foundation built from lived evidence rather than reassurance, hype, or forced positive thinking.

Feelings Are Not Facts and Personal Progress

The same logic applies directly to how people assess their own progress. A preserved record — of what was attempted, handled, learned, and carried through — does not exist to override a difficult feeling or prove that the difficulty was unwarranted. It exists so the feeling is not the only evidence being consulted when a person tries to answer a much larger question than the current mood was ever equipped to answer on its own: what is actually true about how my life has been going. A record like this does not argue with the feeling. It simply makes sure the feeling does not have to stand in for an entire account of a person's life, when it was only ever built to describe a moment.

The Professional Application

Therapists, coaches, and other professionals frequently meet this exact pattern in the room. A client arrives having just lived through a difficult stretch and offers a global verdict shaped by it: I did nothing right this week, I completely failed, I cannot handle any of this. The verdict is delivered with total conviction, because that is how emotional reasoning presents itself: as a settled account of events rather than one possible reading among others.

A preserved record of what actually happened during that period can give both the client and the professional something more specific to examine together than the verdict alone. It does not settle the matter or replace the professional's judgment, and it is not a diagnostic or clinical tool. It offers what this system has consistently described elsewhere: better evidence to think about together, applied here to a very common and very human moment — the point at which a difficult feeling has quietly become a complete account of a person's week, month, or life, when it was never built to carry that much weight on its own.

A Concise Canonical Definition

Feelings Are Not Facts™ is the principle that emotions are real experiences and valuable information, while the conclusions drawn from them should be tested against the evidence of what has actually happened.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are feelings facts?

No, though they are real experiences. A feeling reflects something genuinely happening internally, but the specific conclusion a person draws from that feeling — about themselves, another person, or their life — is a separate claim that needs to be checked against actual evidence rather than accepted automatically.

What does "feelings are not facts" mean?

It means that the intensity or certainty of an emotion does not, by itself, prove that the interpretation attached to that emotion is accurate. The feeling is real; the story built on top of it may or may not match what has actually happened.

Are feelings always accurate?

Feelings accurately reflect that something is being experienced, but the interpretations people build from feelings are not always accurate accounts of external reality. A feeling of failure, for instance, is a real experience that does not necessarily correspond to actual failure.

What is emotional reasoning?

Emotional reasoning is a well-documented pattern in which a person treats the presence of a strong feeling as proof that the belief associated with it must be true — for example, feeling anxious about a situation and concluding that the situation must therefore be dangerous, without separately examining whether that conclusion holds up.

Why do emotions feel true?

Emotional states are experienced directly and immediately, without arriving labeled as an interpretation, which makes them feel like a straightforward report on reality rather than one perspective formed under particular conditions. Mood also shapes what a person notices and recalls in the moment, which can make an emotional interpretation feel confirmed by whatever evidence comes to mind first.

Can feelings be wrong?

The feeling itself is not wrong, since it accurately reflects an internal experience. What can be inaccurate is the conclusion a person draws from that feeling about their circumstances, their capability, or their life as a whole.

Should I trust my feelings?

Feelings are worth taking seriously as information, since they often reflect something real. The question this page raises is whether to let a feeling stand as the only evidence when deciding what is actually true about a situation.

How do I separate feelings from facts?

One approach is to distinguish three things that usually arrive together: the experience itself, the interpretation that seems to follow from it, and the evidence available to test that interpretation. Checking the interpretation against a fuller record of what has actually happened, rather than against the feeling alone, is where the separation actually gets made.

Why do I feel like I am failing when I am making progress?

A difficult mood tends to bring difficult memories and predictions more easily to mind, which can make recent difficulty feel like the entire story. If a fuller record of what has actually been accomplished is not readily available, a temporary feeling can end up standing in for a much longer and more complete account of a person's progress.

How can evidence help me see my life more accurately?

A preserved record of what a person has actually done, handled, and lived through gives a difficult feeling something to be weighed against, rather than letting that feeling serve as the only available account. This does not disprove the feeling; it gives the person more complete information with which to judge the conclusion the feeling seems to support.
